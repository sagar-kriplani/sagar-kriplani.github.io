NEST GMBH · MECHANICAL DESIGN

Project period09/2025 – 09/2026

NeSt: design that works in production

Mechanical assemblies, machine enclosures, sheet-metal and welded structures: connecting CAD development and manufacturing documentation with production and supplier coordination.

Autodesk InventorDFM / DFAProduction drawings
Engineering challenge
The bend-relief slits in an 8 mm steel-sheet part tore during forming, leading to repair welding and rework.
Technical decision
Replace the problematic bend with two parts and a planned welded joint.
Result
Repair welding and rework at the torn reliefs were eliminated. The planned welded joint remained part of the design.
Sources & evidence
Methodology, evidence & limitations

  1. 01Engineering problem

    An 8 mm steel-sheet part tore at the bend-relief slits during fabrication. Repair welding and rework made the original one-piece approach unsuitable.

  2. 02Requirements & constraints

    Preserve the component’s function while choosing a fabrication route the workshop could deliver reliably. The example is simplified to protect the actual geometry and interfaces.

  3. 03Technical analysis

    Reviewed where the relief slits tore and the repair work that followed the bend. The relevant trade-off was one formed part versus two parts with a planned welded joint.

  4. 04Design decision

    Replace the problematic bend with a two-part welded construction. Make the intended joint part of the design rather than relying on repair welding after a failed forming operation.

  5. 05Implementation

    Reconsidered the part split and revised the construction to two welded parts. The schematic omits proprietary geometry and weld dimensions.

  6. 06Result & design guideline

    Repair welding and rework caused by the torn bend relief were eliminated. Planned joining remained. Guideline: review bend-relief geometry and fabrication feasibility before release; consider a deliberate part split when forming creates avoidable repair work.

Broader role scope: Developed CAD assemblies, sheet-metal details, welded structures, production drawings, and BOMs. Coordinated design interfaces with production and suppliers, using SAGE ERP and revision-controlled documentation.

Brooks: Process Air Unit for EUV pod cleaning

Process-air conditioning as an equipment-development problem: consider heater sizing, pressure loss, component selection and mechanical packaging together.

ThermodynamicsMechanical layoutComponent selection
Engineering challenge
Simplify an oversized, component-heavy heating arrangement while meeting the analysed process-air duty.
Technical decision
Consolidate the heating arrangement, simplify the flow path and select components for the analysed requirement.
Result
Design comparison: 48 → 18 kW, approximately 500 → 50 mbar pressure drop and around €4,200 lower PAU cost. Not a measured operating-energy saving.
Sources & evidence
Methodology, evidence & limitations

  1. 01Engineering problem

    The existing Process Air Unit for an EUV pod cleaner combined excess heater design capacity, pressure losses and a complex mechanical layout.

  2. 02Requirements & constraints

    Supply the required heated process air while fitting the available equipment space. Consider flow, pressure/temperature requirements, component interfaces, fabrication and integration without disclosing proprietary geometry.

  3. 03Technical analysis

    Compared the air-heating requirement with the installed heater capacity; assessed pressure losses and component selection. The report includes pipe/flange design checks and a comparative component-cost calculation.

  4. 04Design decision

    Consolidate the heating arrangement and simplify the flow path rather than retain an oversized, component-heavy layout. Select a heater sized for the analysed duty and reduce unnecessary transitions and fittings.

  5. 05Implementation

    Developed the mechanical arrangement, component selection and design documentation. The public schematic shows only the process logic; employer CAD and equipment photographs remain excluded.

  6. 06Documented result

    The report compares 48 → 18 kW heater design capacity and approximately 500 → 50 mbar pressure drop. It reports about €4,200 lower PAU cost, approximately 21%. These are report-based design/cost comparisons; capacity reduction is not measured operating-energy saving.

EAH JENA / BROOKS AUTOMATION · MASTER’S THESIS

Project period07/2023 – 01/2024

Brooks / EAH Jena: FOUP heating in vacuum

Interdisciplinary development connecting mechanics, materials and measurement: compare radiant heaters and IR LEDs for heating FOUP polymers.

Experimental designFTIR / UV-VisThermal measurement
Engineering challenge
Determine whether IR LEDs meet the tested heating duty across materials with different optical absorption.
Technical decision
Combine spectroscopy with comparative heating tests; assess temperature distribution alongside the measurement limitations.
Result
Radiant heaters remained preferable for the tested material range and overall heating duty. LEDs offered more uniform heating, with limits in transparent polymers and heat rejection.
Sources & evidence
Methodology, evidence & limitations

  1. 01Research question

    Could infrared LEDs replace radiant heaters for polymer heating within FOUP-cleaner vacuum chambers, across materials with different optical absorption?

  2. 02Requirements & test conditions

    Compare black EBM and transparent polycarbonate under the stated test cycle and source-to-surface distances. The methodology reports 20 °C ambient and a 0.03 kPa vacuum condition; no independent chamber log was supplied.

  3. 03Technical analysis

    Used FTIR and visible/near-IR absorption measurements to compare spectral response and select 940 nm LEDs. Evaluated surface-temperature observations and distribution, not electrical input alone.

  4. 04Engineering decision

    Conventional radiant heaters remained preferable for the tested overall heating duty and material range. LEDs showed more uniform heating and potential for selective absorption, but transparent polymers and LED heat rejection limited substitution.

  5. 05Experimental implementation

    Developed mechanical supports and vacuum-compatible electrical integration for comparative source arrangements. Used post-cycle infrared-camera readings and irreversible temperature strips. These are not continuous in-situ temperature measurements.

  6. 06Findings & limitations

    The source plots below show the tested black-polymer response. Treat nominal power × time as an electrical-input comparison, not absorbed thermal energy or full-system efficiency. Venting/cooling before measurement, emissivity, LED heat dissipation and short test duration limit the conclusions.
